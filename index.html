Volume I — The Thesis

A framework, in three movements.

Tier Two is not a hedge against the metropolis; it is its successor. We read it as a sequence — what it is, what is being built, why now — and let the cities themselves serve as proof.

01 / Information
What, precisely, is a Tier Two city?

A Tier Two city, in the language of Indian planners, is a place between half a million and a million in population — large enough to sustain economy, small enough to remain legible. They are the cities one drives through on the way to somewhere; soon, they will be the destinations themselves.

They share a rhythm: a metro line announced; a logistics corridor laid; a land record digitised. Each is unremarkable on its own. Together, they describe an emerging geography of demand — quieter, less photographed, considerably less priced-in.

02 / Development
What is, at this hour, being built.

The Indian state is, for the first time at scale, investing in the periphery. Eight expressways under construction cross Tier Two terrain. Metros extend past the older ring roads. Industrial parks anchor what were, ten years ago, agricultural taluks.

Capital follows infrastructure with a delay measured in years, not decades. The window between the announcement and the absorption is precisely the window we mean to inhabit — neither speculative, nor late.

03 / Opportunity
Why this, and why now.

Tier One is mature; its land has been priced for two decades by people who saw what was coming. Tier Two is at the inflection — the moment after the line on the map is drawn, and before the photographs of it appear in the brochures.

For the patient buyer with a horizon of seven years and a preference for the considered over the conspicuous, Tier Two India is the most uncommon opportunity available at scale; a rare alignment of demography, policy, and price.

Volume I — The Holiday Home Framework

Six pillars, written for Lansdowne.

A second home, well-considered, is built on six legs. None alone is sufficient; together they describe whether the place will hold its meaning over time. We test each, in turn, against the hill station that prompted the question.

I — Tourism

A reason for arrival.

A holiday home is anchored, first, by the reason a stranger would come. Footfall sustains amenity; amenity sustains value. The cantonment town receives a steady, undramatic tourism — the kind that does not exhaust a place.

II — Rental

Yield without absence.

The home pays for itself between visits — weekend nights, long weekends, festival seasons. The arithmetic is plain: short-let rates, multiplied by realistic occupancy, against a measured cost of holding.

III — View

Geography, irreplaceable.

A view is not built; it is inherited. At seventeen hundred metres, with the ridges of Garhwal at the horizon and the Himalayas visible on a clear morning, the asset is the geography itself.

IV — Appreciation

Value, by the decade.

Hill towns appreciate slowly and unspectacularly, and they do so for reasons cement cannot replicate. Cantonment protection caps supply; the road from Delhi shortens; the buyer arrives.

V — Second Home

A house, kept against the year.

The principal use case remains, of course, one's own. A four-hour drive from Delhi; a place to write the year's harder paragraphs; a household belonging to two cities, neither lesser for the other.

VI — Infrastructure

Roads, water, network.

A hill home is a function of the road that reaches it. The Delhi–Kotdwar corridor has been steadily widened; mobile networks reach the cantonment cleanly; water, in this part of the Garhwal, is the unremarkable kind one stops noticing.

Chapter I — The Inflection

A plain that has turned its corner.

For three decades, Bahadurgarh was the place between Delhi and somewhere further. Today, by every measurable line — population, transit, land — it is itself the destination.

Bahadurgarh sits at twenty-eight kilometres west of Connaught Place, on the National Highway that leaves Delhi for Rohtak; in administrative terms, the gate of Haryana. For the better part of the last century, this was a town one drove through; the houses on the highway were modest, the agricultural belt extended close to the road, and the rhythm was unhurried.

The change was, characteristically, infrastructural. The Delhi Metro's Green Line — extended after years of fitful planning — reached Bahadurgarh in 2018. A direct, cool, air-conditioned ride to Inderlok and onward to the rest of the network arrived. Property listings, which had crept upward through the 2010s, accelerated. The town's centre, around the new metro stations, began to consolidate as a small, but real, urban node.

Two further pieces of infrastructure now bracket the case. The KMP Expressway — Kundli-Manesar-Palwal — passes within a short drive, threading Bahadurgarh into the logistics ring around Delhi. The NH-9 widening continues. And the population, which crossed six lakhs in the most recent count, is composed largely of working households who have moved out from the inner city by deliberate choice.

The figure most often cited is the thirty-five-fold appreciation of land prices since 2006. The figure is roughly accurate, and roughly meaningless without context: the appreciation is the consequence of metro access, of regional connectivity, and of a singular accident of geography that places Bahadurgarh on the only direct rail line to Delhi from the west of the NCR.

28KM Connaught Place — a thirty-minute drive on the highway.
2018 Metro Green Line — direct rail to inner Delhi.
6L+ Population — sustained working households.

Chapter II — Then & Now

Two pages, twenty years apart.

A town described in 2006, as it then read; and again in 2025, as it now reads. The pages, set facing, do most of the speaking themselves.

2006
A town on the highway out.
  • Land at agricultural rates; transactions infrequent and largely local.
  • No direct public transit to Delhi; private cars and shared vans.
  • Small, family-held plots; few institutional developers present.
  • Population steadily growing; the city itself, by any urban measure, modest.
  • Services and schooling drawn, mostly, from Delhi or Rohtak.
2025
A node in the metropolitan grid.
  • Direct metro access since 2018; expansion to the north and west underway.
  • KMP Expressway and NH-9 corridor logistics belt active.
  • Six-lakh-plus population; tier-two classification met by every measure.
  • Plotted developments, premium and otherwise, now common; supply structured.
  • Land prices roughly thirty-five times those of two decades ago.

The Green Line, drawn in.

A short diagrammatic sketch of the Delhi Metro Green Line as it reaches Bahadurgarh — the single most consequential line drawn through this part of the NCR.

A direct, air-conditioned ride from the heart of Delhi — the corridor that, for our purposes, redrew the map.

Chapter IV — The Investment Case

Three reasons, in plain prose.

The argument for entering Bahadurgarh now, written without dramatics. Each item below is one paragraph, neither more nor less than is required.

i.
Delhi overflow,
now structural.
The capital has, for some years, been gently ejecting its working middle class to its periphery. Bahadurgarh receives that flow on the western side. Households that work in Delhi, school in Delhi, and bank in Delhi increasingly sleep in Bahadurgarh — and increasingly own there.
ii.
Singular metro access,
not soon repeated.
The Green Line, in this stretch, is the only direct rail link west of Delhi. The next phase of metro expansion is allocated, by current planning, to other directions. The advantage Bahadurgarh holds — a thirty-minute commute by train — will not, for several years, be matched by any neighbouring town.
iii.
A pricing window
still open.
Despite the appreciation, plotted land in Bahadurgarh's premium pockets remains a fraction of comparable Gurugram or Faridabad lots. The arbitrage, in our view, will close over the next three to seven years; the window for an unhurried entry remains, for now, ajar.

Chapter I — The Six Pillars

Six legs, tested at altitude.

The Holiday Home Framework, applied paragraph by paragraph to the place that prompted it. Each pillar appears below in its own modest spread, with neither more weight nor less than the argument requires.

I
Tourism — a steady, undramatic season.

Lansdowne does not, in the modern Indian sense, draw crowds. It does not have a mall road of the Mussoorie type; it has, instead, a chowk and a tarred lane and the regimental museum of the Garhwal Rifles. Its tourism is composed largely of weekenders from Delhi, of families seeking quiet, of riders pausing on longer Himalayan routes.

This is, for our purposes, the ideal kind of footfall. It is steady through the months of October to April; it is unaccompanied by the seasonal collapse of infrastructure that troubles the larger hill stations; and it generates, for a properly held second home, a defensible rental ledger.

II
Rental — yield, by the weekend.

The arithmetic of a Lansdowne rental is not extravagant; it is ordinary, and ordinary, in this category, is the better promise. Two-bedroom homes in the cantonment vicinity let, on the short-stay platforms, in a band of seven to fourteen thousand rupees per night.

Realistic occupancy — twelve to twenty weekends, plus the longer holiday windows — produces a gross figure that, against a measured cost of caretaking, leaves a household substantially closer to break-even than most second homes in India achieve.

III
View — the asset that cannot be reproduced.

From the eastern ridges of the cantonment, the Garhwal Himalayas — Trishul, Nanda Devi on the clearest mornings — rise across the horizon. The view is, in the most literal economic sense, the asset; cement does not replicate it, and no amount of construction below alters it.

Plots with line-of-sight to the snow line have, in our experience, held a premium of forty per cent over their valley-facing peers, and they have widened the gap, not narrowed it, with the years.

IV
Appreciation — slowly, and for reasons.

Hill towns appreciate not in the manner of metros — by spurts of euphoria — but by slow, almost geological accretion. A road is widened; a hospital opens a wing; a school is announced. Each event is small. The cumulative effect, over a decade, is considerable.

The cantonment's structural restraint on supply — discussed at length below — converts even modest demand growth into measurable price movement. Lansdowne has no oversupply, and no architectural mechanism by which oversupply can quickly arrive.

V
Second Home — a household, in two cities.

A four-hour drive from Delhi places Lansdowne squarely within the working weekend. One leaves on Friday after lunch and arrives in time for dinner; one returns on Sunday evening, with the city's air still on one's clothes. The home, in this sense, is not an expedition; it is an alternation.

It is the kind of place where, over a decade, one's children remember a particular tree by name; where one writes the year's harder paragraphs; where the household, taken whole, belongs to two cities — neither lesser for the other.

VI
Infrastructure — roads, water, network.

The Delhi–Kotdwar corridor has been steadily widened over the last decade; the final stretch up the hill is being progressively repaired. Mobile networks reach the cantonment cleanly; broadband, since the pandemic, has become unremarkable. Water, in this part of the Garhwal, is the unremarkable kind one stops noticing.

None of this is glamorous. None of it appears in the brochures of competing destinations. It is, however, the substrate on which the rest depends — and Lansdowne's substrate has, with some patience, become quietly competent.

Chapter II — A Brief History

A century, at altitude.

Lansdowne has been, for one hundred and thirty-eight years, the same town. The dates below trace the small movements that have, in their accumulation, kept it so.

1887

Founding, by Lord Lansdowne.

Established as a recruitment depot for the Garhwal Rifles by the Viceroy whose name it then took; held, from the first, under cantonment jurisdiction.

1920s

A small civilian population settles.

Bungalows arrive on the ridgelines above the cantonment; the regimental life is joined by a modest civilian one. The town's character — restrained, dignified, aware of itself — settles into place.

1947

Independence; the cantonment endures.

Where many British-era towns underwent rapid post-war transformation, Lansdowne, anchored by the regiment that remained in place, continued largely as before. Its scale was, by accident and by law, preserved.

2000s

A second-home market begins to form.

As the Delhi corridor improved and the larger hill stations grew crowded, a generation of buyers began to look for the smaller, quieter alternative; Lansdowne, neither overlooked nor advertised, took its place on the list.

Now

A measured, considered moment.

Today, with the Delhi–Kotdwar road materially improved, with broadband at altitude, and with the cantonment's restraints intact, the conditions for a second home are, by almost every measure, as good as they have been in a generation.

Chapter III — An Estimate

An estimate, in plain prose.

No calculator with sliders or graphs. A single sentence, with two thin underlined inputs; the figure updates, quietly, as you write.

If your home is rented for weekends each year, at an average rate of ₹ per night, the indicative annual gross is ₹2,88,000.

— a working figure only; assumes two nights per weekend, taxes and operating costs not deducted, and presumes the home is held in a configuration suitable for short-stay use.

Chapter IV — Lansdowne, not Mussoorie

Two columns, read together.

Set against its more populous neighbours, Lansdowne's case is, very simply, that it is what those places used to be. The columns below put the matter side by side.

Lansdowne.

— the considered choice
  • Cantonment-protected: supply restrained by law for a hundred and twenty-five years.
  • Population at the scale of a village; the sky, at night, still legible.
  • Four hours from Delhi by a now-widened road.
  • Seventeen hundred metres; cool through summer, properly cold in winter.
  • A steady, dignified tourism that does not exhaust the place.

Mussoorie · Shimla · Nainital.

— the crowded familiar
  • No structural restraint on supply; private development, broadly, unbounded.
  • Town populations that have, for two decades, been outgrowing the infrastructure.
  • Drive times affected, from May to July, by considerable congestion.
  • Pricing, by the square foot, in many pockets at metro-equivalent rates.
  • A high-volume tourism that materially alters the experience of residence.
Hold a place in the cantonment.
